Why Giant Teams Are Shrinking: Ronald Coase and the Rise of Tiny High-Margin Firms

In 1937, future Nobel laureate Ronald Coase asked why large corporations exist at all. Combining Coase's transaction-cost theory with Maximilien Ringelmann's 1913 rope-pulling experiments and Fred Brooks's 1975 communication math, this essay explains why tiny high-margin firms are outperforming bloated teams.

Why Industrial Scale No Longer Requires Industrial Headcount

For most of the twentieth century, serious business revenue required serious headcount. If you wanted to serve a hundred thousand customers, you leased three office floors, staffed billing and fulfillment departments, and hired layers of middle managers to supervise them. Today, tiny teams of one to three builders routinely run software tools, research publications, and digital product catalogs generating millions in high-margin revenue without a single conference room. That shift is not a fluke. In 1937, a twenty-six-year-old British economist named Ronald Coase published an essay in Economica that predicted the exact conditions under which giant firms shrink.

  • 20th-century revenue growth demanded layers of middle managers
  • Today, 1-to-3-person digital firms generate multi-million margins
  • One 1937 economics paper explains the structural shift

Source: Ronald Coase, 'The Nature of the Firm', Economica (1937)

Firms Exist Only Where Open Markets Are Expensive to Use

Coase earned the 1991 Nobel Prize in Economics by asking a deceptively simple question: if free markets coordinate prices so well, why do salaried corporations exist at all instead of independent specialists trading contracts? His answer was transaction costs. In 1937, hiring outside help for every step meant weeks spent locating trustworthy suppliers, drafting paper agreements, and inspecting deliveries by hand. Bringing workers onto a permanent payroll eliminated that constant haggling. Yet Coase added a crucial boundary condition: a firm should expand only until internal management friction costs as much as using outside tools and markets.

Source: Ronald Coase, 'The Nature of the Firm', Economica (1937); Nobel Prize Lecture (1991)

The Combinatorial Math That Slows Growing Teams Down

Internal management friction compounds faster than most founders realize, and two classic studies measure the drag. In 1913, French agricultural engineer Maximilien Ringelmann measured volunteers pulling a heavy rope alone versus in groups. As group size climbed toward eight, individual force dropped sharply due to mistimed coordination and diffused ownership. Six decades later, in his 1975 classic The Mythical Man-Month, IBM engineering director Fred Brooks showed why: communication channels grow by N times N minus one, divided by two. Four builders manage just six links. Fifteen people must maintain one hundred and five links, starving deep execution.

  • Ringelmann (1913): per-person rope pull fell steeply in groups of 8
  • Brooks (1975): 4 people = 6 links, 15 people = 105 links
  • Adding headcount multiplies status alignment faster than output

Source: Maximilien Ringelmann, Annales de l'Institut National Agronomique (1913); Fred Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month (1975)

What Happens to Coase's Boundary When Code Does the Routing

Now plug modern software infrastructure into Coase's 1937 equation. Global payment rails handle multi-currency taxes and checkout in ten lines of code. Cloud servers scale overnight traffic without an internal IT floor. Python pipelines and AI workflows transcribe, format, test, and package digital assets in minutes without scheduling a cross-departmental sync. Because external transaction costs have fallen toward zero while human coordination taxes inside large meetings remain just as steep as in Ringelmann's day, the optimal Coasean boundary of a firm shrinks dramatically. Small, technically literate operators capture enterprise-scale distribution without carrying enterprise overhead.

Source: Structural synthesis of Coase (1937) and Brooks (1975)

Stop Confusing Crowded Org Charts With Real Leverage

Despite this math, ambitious builders still walk straight into the headcount trap because social status lags behind economic reality. At industry dinners, saying you employ thirty people sounds impressive, even if payroll eats ninety-two percent of revenue and turns your calendar into forty hours of personnel mediation. Compare that life with a focused one-or-two-person firm earning nine hundred thousand dollars at an eighty-two percent margin. The smaller founder takes home more net cash, carries near-zero fixed survival stress during slow quarters, and spends mornings refining products instead of refereeing Slack disputes.

Apply the 3-Filter Coasean Leverage Audit This Week

Run every bottleneck in your work or business through this three-filter Coasean audit before adding headcount. Filter One is Subtraction: eliminate bespoke, low-margin offerings that generate endless client handoffs, and standardize around repeatable assets. Filter Two is Deterministic Code: whenever you repeat an operational step three times, turn it into a checklist, template, or automated script so execution happens with zero calendar friction. Filter Three is Modular Contracting: when you genuinely need outside expertise, buy clearly specified finished deliverables from top independent specialists rather than hiring permanent layers of supervision. Stay small, stay sharp, and bank the margin.

  • 1. Subtract: cut custom one-off offers that create coordination chaos
  • 2. Script: automate any deterministic task repeated 3+ times
  • 3. Spec Out: buy modular deliverables, not full-time management load
