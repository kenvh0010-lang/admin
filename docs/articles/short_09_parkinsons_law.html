Parkinson's Law: Why Tighter Deadlines Can Improve Your Work

In 1955, historian C. Northcote Parkinson wrote a famous satirical essay in The Economist: work expands to fill the time available. This video shows how shorter deadlines can cut the fluff.

Why 2-Week Tasks Take Exactly 2 Weeks

Give yourself two weeks to finish a project and it will mysteriously take fourteen days. Give yourself two hours before catching a flight, and you finish it with ten minutes to spare.

  • Give a report 14 days and it bloats into 14 days
  • Give it 2 hours before a flight and you ship on time

C. Northcote Parkinson's 1955 Discovery

In a 1955 essay in The Economist, historian Cyril Northcote Parkinson pointed out that between 1914 and 1928, the Royal Navy's capital ships fell by about two-thirds, while Admiralty officials grew by seventy-eight percent.

  • Royal Navy capital ships fell ~67% (1914 to 1928)
  • Admiralty officials rose ~78% in the same years

Loose Deadlines Breed Fake Complexity

Long deadlines rarely create masterpieces; they invite perfectionism, anxious second-guessing, and decorative busywork. Tight constraints force your brain to execute only what moves the needle.

Two Fast Iterations Beat One Slow Draft

That is why a shorter deadline often helps: shipping version one in three days instead of two weeks lets real feedback guide version two.

Set Artificial Micro-Deadlines Today

Take your most important task tomorrow, slice the time budget in half, and start a visible timer. Watch how fast the fluff disappears.

  • Take your top weekly goal and cut its timeline in half
  • Use a visible countdown timer to lock out distractions
