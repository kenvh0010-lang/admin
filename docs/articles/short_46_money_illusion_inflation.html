Why a 5% Raise Can Still Leave You Poorer

Economist Irving Fisher coined 'The Money Illusion' in 1928, and Eldar Shafir, Peter Diamond, and Amos Tversky proved in a 1997 Quarterly Journal of Economics paper that our brains fixate on nominal dollar figures while ignoring real purchasing power. Here is the fix.

When a 5% Pay Bump Is Actually a 1% Pay Cut

Why does a five-percent salary raise during six-percent inflation feel like a victory, even though your real purchasing power just shrank?

  • Paystub shows more dollars hitting checking
  • Grocery and rent bills quietly outpace the raise

Source: Irving Fisher, The Money Illusion (1928); Shafir, Diamond & Tversky, QJE (1997)

Irving Fisher (1928) & Tversky's 1997 QJE Proof

Economist Irving Fisher named this Money Illusion in 1928. In 1997, Eldar Shafir, Peter Diamond, and Amos Tversky proved in the Quarterly Journal of Economics that people celebrate bigger nominal raises despite smaller real gains.

  • Fisher (1928): people mistake dollar units for static value
  • Shafir, Diamond & Tversky (1997): +5% in 4% inflation beat +2% in 0%

Source: Shafir, Diamond & Tversky, 'Money Illusion', Quarterly Journal of Economics (1997)

Idle Cash Looks Safe While Losing Real Ground

Watching raw dollar numbers tricks savers into leaving large balances in near-zero checking accounts, smiling at a steady number while inflation quietly erodes buying power.

Subtract Inflation Before Celebrating Any Gain

Think strictly in real terms: subtract inflation from every salary bump, savings yield, and investment return before deciding whether your household actually moved ahead.

Score Real Purchasing Power, Not Screen Digits

Track real purchasing power after inflation, never flattering nominal numbers on a bank screen.

  • Move idle cash buffers into competitive real-yield accounts
  • Benchmark career and asset growth against real living costs
