Why Meetings Spend Hours on Fonts and Minutes on Strategy

In his 1957 book Parkinson's Law, C. Northcote Parkinson formulated the Law of Triviality: a committee approves a 10-million-pound reactor in 2.5 minutes, then argues 45 minutes over a cheap bike shed. Here is how to stop personal bikeshedding.

2 Minutes on a Reactor, 45 on a Bike Shed

Why does a committee approve a ten-million-pound nuclear reactor in two minutes, then argue for forty-five minutes over a bike shed?

  • 10M-pound reactor approved in 2.5 quiet minutes
  • 350-pound staff bike shed debated for 45 minutes

Source: C. Northcote Parkinson, Parkinson's Law (1957)

Parkinson's 1957 Law of Triviality

Historian C. Northcote Parkinson explained this in 1957 as the Law of Triviality: complex, high-stakes math intimidates people into silence, whereas everyone understands roof shingles and coffee budgets enough to debate endlessly.

  • Reactor math feels too complex to critique safely
  • Everyone understands roof tiles and coffee budgets

Source: C. Northcote Parkinson, 'High Finance, or the Point of Vanishing Interest' (1957)

Personal Bikeshedding Disguises Procrastination

Solo builders fall into personal bikeshedding constantly: spending three weeks tweaking Notion icons, color palettes, and logos while spending ten minutes on distribution or revenue.

Cap Cosmetics at 15 Minutes; Fund the Reactor

Cap every cosmetic bike-shed decision at fifteen minutes on a timer, and force ninety percent of your morning energy onto the core reactor.

Starve Trivial Polish of Your Prime Hours

Starve trivial polish of oxygen so your sharpest hours compound the big levers that actually pay.

  • Use default templates for every reversible detail
  • Spend prime morning energy only on asymmetric math

Source: Mind & Margin rule based on C. Northcote Parkinson (1957)
