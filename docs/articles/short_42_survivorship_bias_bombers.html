Abraham Wald's WWII Bullet-Hole Map: Stop Copying Lucky Outliers

In 1943, mathematician Abraham Wald at Columbia's Statistical Research Group stopped military planners from armoring the bullet-riddled wings of returning bombers, pointing to the missing planes hit in the engines instead. Here is how Survivorship Bias distorts wealth advice.

The 1943 Bullet-Hole Mistake Nearly Everyone Makes

In 1943, military officers inspected bullet-riddled World War Two bombers and nearly armored the exact wrong parts of the plane until a mathematician intervened.

  • Returning planes had wings covered in shrapnel holes
  • Officers wanted extra armor placed right over the holes

Source: Abraham Wald, Statistical Research Group at Columbia University (1943)

Abraham Wald Counted the Planes That Never Returned

Working at Columbia University's Statistical Research Group, mathematician Abraham Wald noticed returning wings were covered in holes while engines were clean. He advised armoring the spotless engines: planes hit there never returned.

  • Planes hit in the wings survived the flight home
  • Planes hit in the engines crashed before reaching the hangar

Source: Abraham Wald, 'A Method of Estimating Plane Vulnerability', SRG Memo (1943)

Copying Visible Winners While Ignoring Silent Graveyards

Financial media commits that exact Survivorship Bias daily, interviewing reckless leverage gamblers or college-dropout billionaires while ignoring thousands of identical bets that went bankrupt.

Always Inspect the Missing Denominator

Before copying any viral wealth or business playbook, inspect the denominator: look at where the missing casualties failed, and optimize for repeatable base rates.

Armor Your Financial Engines First

Armor your engines against ruin first, because compounding only rewards builders who stay in the air.

  • Never risk survival margin on low-probability bets
  • Build durable skills and cash reserves that prevent ruin
