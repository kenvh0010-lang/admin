Why Smart Projects Take Twice as Long as Planned

In 1994, psychologists Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin, and Michael Ross measured why intelligent people systematically underestimate completion times. Here is how Daniel Kahneman's Reference Class Forecasting fixes broken schedules.

Why 'Two-Week' Sprints Turn Into Five Weeks

Why do intelligent, experienced professionals estimate a two-week project timeline and routinely finish five weeks later?

  • Even experienced builders repeat optimistic deadline errors
  • Intelligence alone does not cure schedule blindness

Source: Buehler, Griffin & Ross, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (1994)

Buehler, Griffin & Ross's 1994 Thesis Study

In a 1994 study, psychologists Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin, and Michael Ross asked honors students when their theses would finish. Students predicted thirty-four days on average, yet actual completion took nearly fifty-six.

  • Students predicted 34 days; actual average hit 55.5 days
  • Even their 'worst-case' estimates averaged only 49 days

Source: Buehler, Griffin & Ross, JPSP, Vol. 67, No. 3 (1994)

Inside View Fantasy vs Real-World Friction

Daniel Kahneman traced this planning fallacy to the Inside View: your brain pictures an uninterrupted best-case sprint while ignoring sick days, scope changes, and hidden dependencies.

Source: Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011)

Reference Class Forecasting + 1.5x Buffer

Use Kahneman's Reference Class Forecasting instead: check how long your last three similar projects actually took from start to finish, then multiply your optimistic estimate by one point five.

Source: Lovallo & Kahneman, Harvard Business Review (2003); 1.5x buffer is practical advice

Under-Promise on Dates, Over-Deliver on Craft

Price real-world friction into your calendar upfront so you hit deadlines calmly and protect your reputation.

  • Log actual completion times to calibrate future quotes
  • Calm buffers turn rushed panic into trusted execution

Source: Mind & Margin rule of thumb (advice, not a study)
