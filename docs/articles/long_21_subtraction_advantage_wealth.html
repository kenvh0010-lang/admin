The Power of Subtraction: Why Removing Friction Beats Adding Habits

In 2021, Gabrielle Adams and colleagues showed in Nature across 8 studies that people systematically overlook subtractive improvements, especially under mental load. Combining their findings with Nassim Taleb's Via Negativa and Atul Gawande's Checklist Manifesto, here is how removing friction beats piling on fragile new habits.

The Lego Bridge Blind Spot Published in 'Nature'

In 2021, behavioral scientist Gabrielle Adams, engineer Leidy Klotz, and colleagues at the University of Virginia published a landmark eight-study paper in Nature involving more than fifteen hundred participants. In one experiment, volunteers were asked to stabilize an uneven Lego roof above a small figurine, where every added brick cost ten cents while removing bricks was free. Removing a single corner brick leveled the roof instantly at zero cost, yet the vast majority paid to pile extra bricks underneath. Whether editing essays, fixing soup recipes, or improving travel itineraries, capable adults systematically overlooked subtraction.

  • Adams et al. (2021): 8 experiments on problem solving
  • Participants paid for extra Lego bricks instead of removing 1 for free
  • Even on symmetrical grids and travel plans, subtraction was ignored

Source: Adams, Converse, Hales & Klotz, 'People systematically overlook subtractive changes', Nature (2021)

Cognitive Overload Amplifies the Additive Trap

Adams and her co-authors dug deeper to see why our minds miss obvious deletions. When participants solved grid puzzles while simultaneously tracking scrolling numbers on screen, their reliance on additive solutions grew even more lopsided. Subtraction requires two mental steps: first understanding how an existing structure works, then imagining it without a familiar piece. Only when researchers added an explicit reminder that removing pieces was allowed did subtractive fixes jump significantly. Workplace incentives reinforce the same trap every quarter. Managers win promotions by launching visible new committees, software subscriptions, and Monday reports, whereas quietly canceling an obsolete meeting leaves behind no shiny monument to claim credit for.

Source: Adams et al., Nature (2021), cognitive load and cueing experiments

Eliminating Downside Leaks Beats Chasing Fragile Alpha

Risk scholar Nassim Nicholas Taleb captured the antidote in his 2012 book Antifragile through an ancient philosophical principle called Via Negativa: knowing what to remove is far more reliable than guessing what to add. In personal finance, beginners hunt for exotic stock picks, complex tax structures, and fifth income streams while carrying twenty-percent credit card balances, bloated car payments, and reactive trading habits. Adding a clever investment strategy adds fragility if one bad headline triggers a forced sale. Deleting consumer debt, fixed status overhead, and brokerage apps from your phone raises your net worth with mathematical certainty.

Source: Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder (2012)

How Stripping Complexity Cut ICU Infections by 66%

Surgeon Atul Gawande documented the operational triumph of subtraction in his 2009 book The Checklist Manifesto. Intensive care units are among the most complex environments on earth, packed with monitors, specialists, and thick procedural binders. Yet critical-care researcher Peter Pronovost did not reduce lethal line infections across Michigan hospitals by buying million-dollar diagnostic machines. Instead, he stripped dense medical manuals down to a five-step checklist covering handwashing, sterile draping, and removing unnecessary catheters promptly. Within eighteen months, ten-day central-line infection rates fell by sixty-six percent simply because crucial basics stopped getting buried under noise.

  • Atul Gawande (2009): modern experts fail from overload, not ignorance
  • Peter Pronovost distilled 100-page protocols into 5 basic steps
  • Zero new hardware required to slash infection rates across Michigan

Source: Atul Gawande, The Checklist Manifesto (2009); Pronovost et al., NEJM (2006)

Why Deleting Friction Outperforms Piling On Willpower

Notice how this changes personal productivity. Every habit you add carries daily maintenance overhead: another box to check, another streak to feel guilty about breaking when travel or illness hits. Subtractive design carries zero ongoing upkeep once installed. Putting your phone charger in the hallway removes ninety minutes of late-night scrolling without requiring midnight discipline. Unsubscribing from retail promo emails removes fifty spending temptations a year in three clicks. Closing every browser tab except your active manuscript eliminates context-switching residue before your morning writing sprint even begins, letting ordinary effort travel twice as far.

Run the 3-Part Via Negativa Audit Tonight

Put subtraction to work tonight with a thirty-minute three-part audit. First, run Financial Via Negativa: open your last ninety days of bank statements, cancel three forgotten subscriptions or low-joy recurring expenses, and route every freed dollar into an automatic index transfer on payday. Second, install a Calendar Minus-One Rule: never say yes to a new recurring meeting, committee, or side project without naming the existing commitment you are retiring first. Third, replace sprawling daily task apps with a five-line Gawande card for your morning focus block, centered on shipping one durable asset while keeping your life remarkably light.

  • 1. Financial Via Negativa: cut 3 recurring charges -> auto-invest 100%
  • 2. Calendar Minus-One Rule: drop 1 old commitment before accepting 1 new
  • 3. Five-Line Morning Card: strip your deep block to 1 shippable asset
